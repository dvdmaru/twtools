2026 Fresh Graduate Job Hunt Guide: How to Write Your First Resume With No Experience, Plus Interview and Starting Salary Tips

Workplace Skills · May 26, 2026 · 10 min

Graduation season is here, and sending out your first-ever resume and walking into your first interview is nerve-wracking — that’s completely normal. For fresh graduates, the biggest hurdle in the job hunt usually isn’t the lack of experience itself; it’s not knowing how to clearly explain what you already have to offer. This guide shows you how to write a resume with real highlights even without experience, avoid the red flags that get you instantly filtered out by HR, what to prepare before an interview, and — building on Taiwan’s 2026 minimum wage of NT$29,500 — how to confirm the salary and working conditions for your first job. (Data current as of May 2026.)

With no experience, what should go in your first resume?

No full-time work history doesn’t mean you have nothing to write about. What HR wants to see is “can you do this job?” — so include the following, as long as it’s relevant to the role:

  • Internships, part-time jobs, and class projects: even if it was just part-time work, spell out what you were responsible for and what results you produced. For example, “Organized a 500-record client list and helped improve the repeat-purchase rate.”
  • Student organizations and leadership roles: running events, managing budgets, and coordinating a team are all skills that transfer to the workplace.
  • Skills and certifications: language ability (such as a TOEIC score), software tools (Excel, design software, programming languages), and relevant professional certifications.
  • Class projects or a portfolio: for engineering, design, and marketing majors, attaching a link to your work or an explanation of your results is the most persuasive thing you can do.

The key is to let numbers and results do the talking, rather than just listing the things you did. The less experience you have, the more you need to articulate the value of your academic and extracurricular background. The 3 biggest resume red flags HR rejects on sight

Many fresh graduates aren’t losing out on ability — they’re losing out on these small details:

  • Typos and messy formatting: spelling mistakes, inconsistent full-width and half-width punctuation, and uneven font sizes all make you look careless. Always proofread before sending, and you can run it through a Chinese Typo Checker first.
  • Sending one resume to everyone: using the same resume for every role, regardless of the position, tells HR right away that you didn’t put in the effort. At a minimum, adjust the focus of your “personal statement” and “skills” sections for different roles.
  • A personal statement that reads like a diary: stop opening with lines like “I come from a warm and loving family.” Get straight to “why I’m a good fit for this job” and “which relevant experiences I have,” and save your space for what matters.

The goal of a resume isn’t to write a complete autobiography — it’s to make HR want to interview you. One to two pages with clear key points is ideal; there’s no need to cram it full.

5 things you must do before an interview

Spending an hour or two preparing before an interview makes a huge difference in how you perform on the spot:

  • Research the company: look at the official website, the products, and recent news, and prepare a specific answer for “why I want to join this company.”
  • Prepare your self-introduction: practice both a 1-minute and a 3-minute version, hit the key points, don’t recite from a script — natural is best.
  • Think through the common questions: work out your answers to “your biggest strengths and weaknesses,” “why you chose this job,” and “your future plans” in advance, so you don’t freeze up in the moment.
  • Prepare questions to ask back: interviews usually end with “do you have any questions?” You can ask about the work itself, the team, and growth opportunities — don’t only ask about salary and time off.
  • Confirm the time, place, and dress code: arrive 10–15 minutes early; for online interviews, test your equipment and connection beforehand; clean and tidy attire is enough — no need to over-stress about it.

How do you negotiate your first salary? Start by understanding 2026 starting-pay benchmarks

Taiwan’s 2026 minimum wage has been raised to a monthly NT$29,500 and an hourly NT$196 (effective January 1, 2026). This is the legal floor — no full-time job’s base salary may fall below this figure.

As for the actual starting salary for fresh graduates, in recent years the initial pay for university graduates has mostly landed between NT$30,000 and NT$35,000 a month, with clear variation by industry, role, and region (tech and finance tend to pay more). Before negotiating, do two things:

  • Look up the salary range for the same role on a job board to get a sense of the market rate, so you have a baseline in mind.
  • Figure out your “target salary” and your “acceptable minimum,” and when asked, give a range rather than a single fixed number.

If the figure they offer is clearly below market and comes with no other compensation (bonuses, learning opportunities, flexible hours), you can politely express your expectations and explain your reasoning — but don’t ask for the moon either. For a fresh graduate’s first job, “room to grow” is sometimes more worth weighing than the starting salary figure.

Working conditions you must confirm before your start date (probation period, labor and health insurance, overtime pay)

When you get an offer, don’t rush to accept. Ask about these items first, and ideally get them in writing in your employment contract:

  • Probation period: the law doesn’t mandate a probation period, but 1–3 months is common in practice. Confirm the probation-period salary, whether you’ll be legally enrolled in labor and health insurance, and the criteria for passing.
  • Labor and health insurance and labor pension: for full-time employees, from the first day on the job the employer is required by law to enroll you in labor insurance and health insurance and to contribute 6% to your labor pension. If a company says “we’ll insure you after you pass probation,” that’s illegal — be especially careful.
  • Working hours and overtime pay: confirm normal working hours, how overtime is calculated, and whether there’s overtime pay or compensatory time off. Under the Labor Standards Act, for the first 2 hours of extended weekday work the rate is +1/3, and from the 3rd hour onward it’s +2/3.
  • Salary structure: ask clearly how much the “base salary,” “perfect-attendance bonus,” and “performance bonus” each are, because labor and health insurance, overtime pay, and severance pay are mostly calculated based on your base salary or regular wages — stuffing your pay into variable bonuses isn’t necessarily in your favor.

FAQ

What is Taiwan’s 2026 minimum wage?

A monthly NT$29,500 and an hourly NT$196, effective January 1, 2026. No full-time job’s base salary may fall below this figure, and hourly part-time work must also reach NT$196 per hour.

Will my resume look bad if I have no work experience?

No. Present your internships, part-time jobs, student organizations, class projects, certifications, and skills with “results plus numbers,” and tailor the focus to the role — you can write a persuasive resume even without full-time experience.

Can a company skip labor and health insurance for me during probation?

No. By regulation, full-time employees must be enrolled in labor insurance and health insurance, with labor pension contributions, from the first day on the job. “We’ll insure you only after probation” is an illegal practice — watch for it and speak up if you encounter it.

When the interview ends with “do you have any questions?”, what should I ask?

You can ask about the actual scope of the work, the team’s composition, future development, and learning opportunities — it shows initiative and that you’ve done your homework. You can also ask about salary and time off, but don’t ask only about those, or you’ll leave the impression that you only care about the perks.
