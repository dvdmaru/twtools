Japan Solo Travel Guide: Essential Preparation and Practical Tools for Your Trip

Travel Planning · April 7, 2026 · 8 min

Traveling to Japan has become an annual itinerary for many Taiwanese people. Although traveling freely is comfortable, it also requires some preparation in advance so that you won’t be in a hurry when you arrive at the place. I have been to Japan no less than ten times. From the initial confusion to now being able to arrange things properly, I have accumulated a lot of practical experience. Today I’m going to share everything from pre-trip preparations, currency exchange strategies, itinerary planning, to how to deal with various small challenges you will encounter during the trip.

Preparation before traveling: Compare air tickets and accommodation together to find the most cost-effective combination

The first step is to book air tickets and accommodation, but don’t look at them separately, because sometimes the more expensive air tickets and cheaper accommodation can save the total cost.

When comparing air tickets, I usually check them on Skyscanner, Google Flights and the airline’s official website at the same time, because sometimes it’s cheaper to buy directly from the airline (especially when it comes to promotions from airlines like Cathay Pacific and JAL). It’s important to avoid peak times – times like Japan’s cherry blossom season (late March to mid-April) and Golden Week (late April to early May) can see flight prices double. If schedule flexibility allows, flights may be 30% to 50% cheaper by traveling a month in advance or pushing it back to the off-season.

In terms of accommodation, business hotels in Tokyo (such as Toyoko Inn and Daiwa Roynet) cost about NT$3,000 to NT$5,000 per night. The rooms are small but clean and convenient. Airbnb can be more cost-effective in Kyoto and Osaka, especially if you’re traveling with a group of people. But be aware that the cleaning fee of Airbnb in Japan is sometimes higher than the room price. Read the ratings and reviews before placing an order.

Currency exchange strategy: When is the most cost-effective time to exchange for Japanese yen?

This is where many people get into trouble. The cost of currency exchange may be higher than you think, and you may accidentally spend hundreds of Taiwan dollars more.

My strategy is: bring about half in cash and rely on credit cards and IC cards for the rest. Many places in Japan already accept credit cards, but there are still some small shops that only accept cash. IC cards (Suica or Pasmo) are very easy to use in Tokyo and Kansai. They can be used for taking rides or shopping at convenience stores without having to take out your wallet every time.

Bank exchange rates in Taiwan are usually better than those at the airport, so exchange most of your cash at the bank before departure. If you need Japanese yen temporarily when you arrive in Japan, you can withdraw money using an international Visa card at 7-Eleven ATM. The exchange rate is usually good (because the bank will use the business exchange rate of the day), and the handling fee is about 100 to 150 yuan.

Before departure, I will use the “Exchange Rate Conversion” tool on this site to check the latest Japanese yen exchange rate, so that I can estimate how much Japanese yen the Taiwan dollar in hand can be exchanged for. When checking out at a drug store, you can quickly estimate how much NT dollars you will actually have to pay - you don’t have to hold your computer for a long time.

Trip planning: Day arrangements and AI tools save you time

The itinerary planning for independent travel in Japan is a test of strategy. The geographical location, transportation time, and opening hours of attractions must all be considered. If you don’t arrange it well, you may waste a lot of time moving around.

My experience is that if it’s 3 days, it’s recommended to stay in Tokyo only and don’t take the Shinkansen to Kansai; if it’s 5 days, you can add 3 days in Tokyo and 2 days in Kyoto or Osaka; if it’s 7 days, 3 days in Tokyo plus 2 days in Kyoto and 2 days in Osaka is the standard itinerary; for more than 10 days, you can add Hokkaido or the central region. Set aside at least a full day in each city so it doesn’t turn into a marathon of sightseeing.

Planning your own itinerary is actually quite time-consuming, especially checking the location, transportation and business hours of each attraction. This site has a TripCraft AI travel planning tool that can automatically generate recommended itineraries based on your number of days, interests and budget, including attractions, restaurants and transportation routes. The time saved can be used to research must-eats or shopping lists.

Countdown and days calculation before departure

It sounds very basic, but many people don’t understand “how many days are left” and “how many annual leave days will be used up during the trip” before departure. If you leave on April 10th and come back on April 17th, how many days off do you need to take? Have you ever had consecutive days off during this period and can you take one less day off? If the calculation is wrong, it may be confused with the number of days of leave requested by the company.

The “Date Calculator” on this site can accurately calculate the number of travel days and working days. “Countdown Timer” allows you to set a countdown to the departure day - to remind yourself that it’s time to pack your luggage, exchange currency, and confirm your hotel. I usually set a countdown immediately after booking my flight to make the anticipation of departure tangible.

Practical tips on the road: Temperature and unit conversion in one second

When arriving in Japan, there are some differences in life that require quick adaptation. The most common one is temperature judgment.

The first time I went to Tokyo, I saw the weather forecast said 5°C and I had no idea what to wear. I later learned that 5°C is about the temperature required to wear a thick sweater and a coat. At 10°C, a light jacket is enough, at 20°C, long sleeves are enough. But if you’re going to Hokkaido, where minus 10°C is the norm in winter, you’ll need a down jacket and a windproof jacket.

The “Unit Conversion” tool on this site supports common conversions such as temperature, weight, distance, etc. Especially when the suitcase is overweight, you need to quickly know how many kilograms equal how many pounds. It is fastest to directly use tools to convert.

I’d also like to share a little dressing tip: Japan’s indoor heating is very strong, so it’s best to adopt the “onion style” in winter—you can take off your coat when you enter a department store or restaurant, and put it on again when you come out. Much more practical than wearing a super thick piece of clothing.

After returning from travel: Don’t delay in organizing photos

During a week in Japan, you might accumulate 500 to 1,000 photos on your camera and phone. If you don’t sort it out while your memory is clear after you come back, you will put it off until you don’t want to touch it again.

The first step in photo organization is filtering and compression. If you just want to share on social media or create a travel photo album, compressing the photos can significantly reduce the file size while retaining sufficient quality. The “Picture Tool” on this site can compress photos or convert formats in batches (such as converting HEIC taken on iPhone to JPG). It is not a problem to process dozens of photos at a time. Backing up to the cloud or an external hard drive is much faster and saves more space.

My habit is to sort it out on the first weekend back, select the best photos, compress them and upload them to the photo album, and back up the rest of the original photos to an external hard drive. It’s true that the longer you put it off, the less you want to do it.

FAQ

**Q: Is it safe to go to Japan during typhoon season (September to October)? **

A: Typhoons are difficult to predict, but Japan has done a good job in disaster prevention. If the itinerary encounters a typhoon warning, some attractions and transportation will be temporarily adjusted. It is recommended to buy travel inconvenience insurance. Flight cancellation or attraction closure can at least reduce financial losses. Also pay close attention to the weather forecast beforehand.

**Q: How to calculate consumption tax and tax refund? **

A: Japan’s consumption tax is 10% (8% on food). The threshold for tax refund is 5,000 yen (price before tax included) spent in a single store in a single day, and it is easier to get a refund in department stores and large drugstores. Just show your passport when checking out and tell the clerk that you want a tax refund. There are two ways to get a tax refund: deduct the tax directly, or include the tax at checkout and apply for it at the airport before departure.

**Q: Should I buy Suica or Pasmo? **

A: Suica is used in Tokyo and Icoca is used in Kansai. The functions are almost the same. These IC cards can be used for rides, convenience stores and vending machines. If you only go to one city, just buy an IC card for that city. Currently, there are limited channels for purchasing physical cards. It is recommended to confirm whether the digital version of iPhone/Apple Watch is available before departure.

**Q: Is there a big difference in budget if I go alone with friends? **

A: The advantage of going alone is that the itinerary is flexible, but the disadvantage is that the average cost of accommodation is high. My experience is that the cost of going alone will be 20-30% more than if it is shared equally between two people. The main difference lies in the accommodation. But if three or more people share Airbnb, the cost will become very cost-effective. In terms of food, one can easily walk into a small restaurant with only a few seats. Sometimes that is the most authentic food.

Traveling independently in Japan is not as complicated as you think. The key is to do your homework beforehand. From air tickets, accommodation, currency exchange, itinerary planning, to various calculations and conversions during the trip, make good use of online tools to automate these chores, so that you can focus on what is really important - enjoying the journey, taking good photos, and tasting delicious food. I hope this sharing is helpful to you. See you on your next trip to Japan!
